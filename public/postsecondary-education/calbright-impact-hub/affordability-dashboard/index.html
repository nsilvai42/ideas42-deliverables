What we tested

We asked participants to evaluate three distinct fee structures for an online education program. To isolate how the fee’s framing (as opposed to amount) affected reactions, we held all three models at the same expected total cost for a typical completer.

Main finding

No one model is right for everyone — framing and mental models matter as much as the structure itself.

In forced ranking evaluations, PAYG leads on enrollment intent and fairness, while Refund competes on completion motivation. Subscription does not lead in any single dimension, but consistently takes second place across them.

Methods & Sample

Who we studied, how we structured the three fee models, and what to keep in mind when reading the findings.

N = 314 prospective adult learners on Prolific, with intentional California oversampling (n=54). Three fee models, all calibrated to the same $460 total at typical pace (the per-unit CCC rate under California Education Code §75010 × 10 modules).

Survey instrument — five sections, mapped to research questions

  • Section 1 · Mental Models & Reference Points — what category do people place a program like this in? what does that category cost?
  • Section 2 · Baseline WTP, Confidence Drivers, Expense Framing — what would they pay? how do they categorize this spending?
  • Section 3 · Individual Fee Model Evaluations — three models, evaluated in isolation; model order randomized
  • Section 4 · Forced-Choice Comparison + Within-Respondent Ranking — three models evaluated comparatively
  • Section 5 · Demographics — for sample composition + subgroup analysis

Where the $460 comes from
All three models are calibrated to work out to the same out-of-pocket cost.
Under California's Education Code, California Community Colleges charge a per-unit enrollment fee of $46. Assuming an average of 10 modules/units across 2 courses per program, the total program cost at the standard CCC rate is 10 units × $46/unit = $460.

How did each model fare, what drove reactions, and for whom did each work?

We tested three fee structures at the same expected total cost. The results do not point to a single model to adopt as-is. Additional Findings

What else shapes how prospective students interpret the price, program, and enrollment decision?

Four findings that are not fee-model-specific but should shape how Calbright communicates with prospective students: Calbright is not automatically read as a community college; $460 can feel surprisingly low without explanation; cost dominates enrollment decisions but requires trust signals; and external quality signals — accreditation, employer recognition, and earning outcomes — carry the next-most weight after cost.

What this means for messaging

Explain the low pricePair $460 with the CCC fee anchor so low cost does not imply low quality.
Show affordability up frontPut aid availability and clear total cost near the first price mention.
Prove value externallyUse accreditation, employer recognition, and outcomes as trust signals.

Recommendations

What pilot design follows from the findings — and what should Calbright user-test before fielding it?

Primary recommendation: Use PAYG as the broad access layer, then user-test an optional progress incentive before piloting. PAYG is the safest base because it feels fair and low-risk, but the study also shows that completion motivation may require an added incentive layer. The next step is not to pick a pure model, but to test whether a PAYG-based design can add motivation without creating an upfront barrier or punitive pressure.

The tension this recommendation resolves

PAYG has the broadest enrollment appeal and fairness signal — 57% pick it as most likely to enroll, with 4.16 enrollment likelihood and 4.25 fairness. Refund and Subscription reveal motivational mechanisms that PAYG lacks, but those mechanisms also create friction: Refund asks learners to front money, while Subscription can feel like ongoing pressure. The recommendation uses PAYG as the access-safe base and tests whether an optional progress incentive can add completion motivation without importing those barriers. The need for that test is real: 39% pick a different model for motivation than enrollment, and 26% of PAYG-pickers choose Refund as more motivating.

Promising insight: framing may be part of the intervention

This study suggests that framing and language matter a lot. Respondents’ reactions shifted depending on how they categorized the program, how price was anchored, and how each fee model’s mechanism was described. Whatever model Calbright moves forward with, user testing should actively explore how to frame, name, and describe both Calbright and the fee model itself.

  • Frame of reference changes what feels affordable or credible.
  • Model names can signal fairness, pressure, punishment, or progress.
  • User testing should compare language variants, not only fee mechanics.

The incentive should be optional, gain-framed, and designed with safeguards for learners whose pace is shaped by work, caregiving, disability, or other constraints.

The pilot to user-test — PAYG with an optional progress incentive

Pilot design
Pay-As-You-Go with an optional per-module progress discount

Structure: Per-module pricing where learners pay when they complete a module. The user-tested variant would add an optional gain-framed discount or reward for timely progress. The clock resets at the start of each module so one slow module does not permanently penalize the learner. Exact dollar values, timing, pause rules, and safeguards should be co-designed before any pilot.

Why this design: The survey points to three design requirements: preserve PAYG’s access and fairness appeal, add an optional completion incentive PAYG lacks, and avoid the upfront barrier or punitive pressure that made the other models risky. A PAYG-based optional progress incentive is a testable way to combine those lessons.

Who to recruit: Prioritize current applicants and prospective Calbright learners for demand, framing, and enrollment reactions. Use current students as a secondary audience for usability, equity, operational, and support concerns.

Primary success metrics: Enrollment rate + module-by-module completion times.

Key risk to surface in convening: Time-based incentives can disadvantage learners whose pace is shaped by disability, caregiving, work, health, or other constraints. User testing should explore pause rules, hardship accommodations, and whether the incentive feels supportive or punitive.

Marketing implications beyond the pilot

Anchor price in the trust trio — never show $460 alone

Pair price with employer recognition, clear cost upfront, financial-aid availability, and CCC/accreditation language. The price needs a reference frame to feel credible.

Reframe the category in the first line

Use “California community college” early and actively. Prospects do not automatically infer this category, but it helps explain why a legitimate program can cost this little.

Use investment language, not subscription language

Respondents more often framed the program as an investment in their future than as a subscription or bill. Match the dominant mental frame.

Do not sell student support as the enrollment hook

Lead with accreditation, employer recognition, earning outcomes, flexibility, clear cost, and aid. Save support-service messaging for later-stage reassurance.

User testing should include messaging variants because framing, naming, and reference points can change how the same fee structure is perceived.
