Why the three models are all calibrated to $460
All three fee structures are tuned so a learner who completes at a typical pace pays the same $460 total. That number comes from California Education Code §75010, which sets the California Community College per-unit fee at $46. With ~10 competency modules treated as ~1 unit each, the program total is 10 × $46 = $460. This calibration isolates the effect of payment framing (when and how learners pay) from the effect of total price.

Three limitations Calbright should keep in mind

!

Stated preferences ≠ enrollment behavior

All findings measure what respondents say they would do, not what they would actually do at enrollment. The pilot is the bridge from one to the other.
For high-deliberation purchases like education, stated preferences predict behavior better than for impulse buys — but not perfectly.
!

Joint-evaluation effects (documented below)

Refund and Monthly ratings drop when shown after PAYG; PAYG ratings hold or rise. PAYG's headline lead is partly a relative-comparison effect.
In the real world, prospective learners encounter one model at a time. The in-isolation numbers are the more behaviorally relevant signal — see the Joint-evaluation finding in Findings tab.
!

Mental-model mismatch with Calbright's brand

Only 5% of our cold sample spontaneously associates a program like this with a community college. Real-world prospects who've engaged with Calbright's brand will be priming on a different reference set.
Marketing actively shapes priors that this cold sample didn't have.
!

Sample skew vs Calbright applicants

72% White (vs ~38% at Calbright); 33% personal-growth-motivated (vs Calbright's career-driven base).
The CA subsample (n=54) is closer (57% White) and used to sanity-check headline claims. Findings replicate when restricted to career-driven respondents.

Findings — Fee Models

How do prospective students rate the three fee structures — and what shapes those ratings?

Five findings, in the order they build toward the recommendation: (1) PAYG wins enrollment intent but lacks completion incentive; (2) Refund is motivating but polarizing; (3) Monthly is the consensus middle; (4) CA respondents tell a different story; (5) PAYG's headline lead is partly an artifact of how we measured it.

Model popularity sits in tension with what works to motivate completion

PAYG wins enrollment intent — but lacks any structural completion incentive. 57% pick it most-likely, 4.16/5 likelihood, 4.25/5 fairness, only 9% rate it negatively. Yet 1 in 4 PAYG-pickers (and 39% of the full sample) say a different model would do more to motivate completion.

Monthly Subscription — the less extreme middle ground

Evaluated independently, 63% endorse a positive motivational impact (44% "finish faster to save money" + 19% "structure or accountability"). But in forced-choice, only 19% pick Monthly as most-motivating — vs 36% PAYG, 37% Refund. Prospective students are fine with Subscription; they just don't love it enough to pick it head-to-head.

Why Monthly motivates — and why some reject it

The dominant rationale for Monthly's motivation reading: the recurring-cost shape creates implicit pressure to complete faster. 44% explicitly said it would "motivate students to finish faster to save money" — the highest finish-fast share of any model.

The same time pressure motivates some and discourages others. Importantly, the rush-pressure concern comes from outside the Monthly-picker segment (14% in other segments vs 4.8% inside Monthly-pickers) — it's an audience-selection issue, not a product fundamentals issue.

5

PAYG's headline lead is partly an artifact of how we measured it

When respondents evaluated each model individually (Section 3), order of presentation was randomized — and the model shown first scored systematically higher. Refund's enrollment likelihood jumps from 2.91 to 3.80 (d=0.64) when shown first; its positive-motivation endorsement nearly triples. Monthly shows the same pattern at smaller magnitude. PAYG is the only model that doesn't suffer from being shown later. The asymmetry is itself the finding: PAYG looks dominant largely because it gains from being a contrast option while Refund and Monthly lose from being one.

Recommendations

What pilot design follows from the findings — and what should Calbright user-test before fielding it?

One primary recommendation: do not choose a vanilla fee model. Co-design and user-test a stitched Speed-Discount PAYG design before piloting. The data argue against vanilla PAYG because popularity and completion motivation do not fully align, and the joint-evaluation evidence shows head-to-head rankings can inflate PAYG's apparent lead. User-test the stitched design with current Calbright applicants in isolation, not in comparison.

The headline tension this recommendation resolves

PAYG has the broadest enrollment appeal — 57% pick it, 4.16 mean, smallest skeptic group at 9% — but no built-in completion incentive. 39% of respondents pick a different structure when asked "which would motivate you to finish." The pilot below borrows PAYG's structural appeal and grafts on the completion mechanic PAYG is missing.

Recommendation 1 — Don't choose vanilla PAYG. Stitch in a speed incentive.

The data argue against simply piloting vanilla PAYG. First, the popularity-versus-motivation split runs through respondents' answers: the model that wins enrollment intent is structurally missing what would drive completion. Second, the order-effect evidence shows PAYG's headline lead is partly an artifact of joint evaluation — in isolation, the three tested models perform far more similarly than the forced-choice rankings suggest.

The primary recommendation is a deliberate hybrid. Speed-Discount PAYG captures PAYG's structural appeal while grafting on the finish-faster incentive respondents saw in time-based payment. The California signal reinforces this: CA respondents (the closest demographic match to Calbright's actual applicants) tilt away from straight PAYG and toward completion-incentive frames.

Recommendation 2 — User-test before piloting. In isolation, not in comparison.

The order-effect data make this not optional. Refund's mean enrollment likelihood swings from 3.80 (when shown first) to 2.52 (when shown third) on a 1–5 scale — a 1.28-point gap driven entirely by where it sits in the sequence. The forced-choice rankings Calbright will read as "PAYG wins decisively" are inflated for PAYG because Refund and Monthly get penalized as contrast options.

Before locking in the pilot, the stitched Speed-Discount PAYG mechanic should be user-tested with current Calbright applicants — especially Latine, Black, and career-motivated learners under-represented in our Prolific sample — and the candidate model should be tested on its own, not as a menu of three. A user test that asks "what do you think of this model?" generates different signal than "rank these three," and the former is the better proxy for how a real prospective student will encounter Calbright's fee structure on a landing page or in an enrollment counselor conversation.

The pilot — Speed-Discount Pay-As-You-Go

Pilot design
Pay-As-You-Go with a per-module speed discount

Structure: Per-module pricing where the price decreases the faster a learner completes the module, down to a floor. The clock resets at the start of each module — completing module 1 quickly unlocks the discount on module 1 only; module 2's clock starts when module 1 is finished. A learner who takes their time pays the higher per-module price; a learner who finishes within ~1 month pays the floor. The first module is free as an enrollment on-ramp.

Why this design — three legs of survey evidence: (1) Vanilla PAYG won every enrollment-related metric across every demographic subgroup, including the California subsample. (2) Monthly's strongest motivation signal — endorsed by 44% of respondents — was "motivate students to finish faster to save money." This pilot grafts that exact mechanic onto PAYG's structure. (3) The Free-Start PAYG variant we explicitly tested in the survey came back +11pp net-positive — the only model variant in the study that did. We're combining three findings, each with direct survey evidence.

Who to recruit: Open recruitment across Calbright's full prospective base. Vanilla PAYG's appeal held in every subgroup; subgroup effects of the specific speed-discount variant should be re-checked in user testing.

Primary success metrics: Enrollment rate + module-by-module completion times.

Key risk to surface in convening: Time-based price disadvantages learners with disability, caregiving, or work constraints whose pace isn't fully in their control. Equity safeguards (e.g., a documented-accommodation floor price, or a hardship pause that holds the current discount tier) should be agreed before pilot launches.

What this study does NOT support

Do Not
Pilot

Four things the findings argue against

1. Escalating-fee Monthly ($28 rising to $64): tested 33% negative vs 21% positive. Respondents cluster on "prefer flat," "unpredictable cost," and "penalizes slow learners."

2. Effort-based Refund (refund tied to assessment scores rather than completion): tested 26% negative vs 18% positive.

3. Don't lead price marketing with $460 unaccompanied. 30% of respondents land in the quality-suspicion zone at that price. Pair it with aid framing + accreditation + employer-recognition signals.

4. Don't lean on community-college framing alone — only 5% spontaneously associate Calbright with a community college. Use it actively as a category reframe, not as an assumed shared frame.

Marketing implications (beyond the pilot)

Anchor price marketing on the trust trio, never on $460 alone

Top three confidence drivers cluster at 66–67%: employer recognition, clear cost upfront, financial-aid availability. Pair every $460 mention with: (a) "CCC per-unit rate set by California law," (b) employer-recognized credential language, (c) financial-aid pathway.

Reframe the category in the first line of any message

Only 5% spontaneously associate the program with a community college. Make "California community college" the first descriptor every prospect sees — it explains why a legitimate program can cost this little.

Use investment language, never subscription language

63% frame the expense as an investment; 24% as a bill; only 3% as a subscription. Even for the Monthly arm, talk about "monthly investment" rather than "monthly subscription." Match the dominant mental frame.

Don't sell student support services as an enrollment hook

Student support ranks last (1%) as the most-important enrollment factor; mean rank 6.83 of 9. Foreground accreditation, earnings outcomes, and employer recognition in pre-enrollment marketing. Save support-service messaging for the post-enroll experience.

Framing the decision points

1

Convene as co-design, not as sign-off

Calbright may lack infrastructure or regulatory headroom for some pilot features. Pushback at the convening is data, not blocker.
Frame as "let's co-design something that works." Specific dollar values, discount schedules, and floor prices stay open until the convening confirms what Calbright can realistically run.
2

User-test without comparative ranking

The Prolific sample evaluated all three models head-to-head. We saw clear joint-evaluation effects — PAYG's lead grew when shown after the others; Refund's appeal nearly tripled when shown first.
In user testing with current Calbright learners, test the model in isolation — the way a real prospective student would encounter it (the pilot model, or the flat-fee control — not a menu of three).
3

The Prolific study was exploratory — now we have direction

Phase 1 was about discovering which fee structures even merit a pilot. The 39% pref-vs-motivation gap, the CA divergence, the trust-bundle signals — all surfaced through exploration.
User testing in Phase 2 should ask more targeted questions: speed-discount sensitivity, equity safeguard preferences, floor-price reactions.
!

Caveats Calbright should know going in

Sample skews White (72%) and personal-growth-motivated (33%) vs Calbright's more diverse, career-driven base. Findings replicate when restricted to the career-driven subset. Stated preferences ≠ enrollment behavior — the pilot is what closes that gap.
The pilot adds operational complexity beyond a vanilla flat-fee program. The convening step is partly to surface whether the design is feasible inside Calbright's current systems.
